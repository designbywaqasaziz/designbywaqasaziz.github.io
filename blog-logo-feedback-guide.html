HOW TO GIVE LOGO FEEDBACK THAT ACTUALLY IMPROVES THE DESIGN.

By Waqas AzizOctober 30, 20264 min read

Most logo revisions do not fail because the designer lacked skill. They fail because the feedback was vague. "Make it pop" and "I do not love it" give a designer nothing to work with, so the next round guesses again. Good feedback is a skill, and it is one you can learn in a single project.

Judge the concept against the brief, not your taste

Before you react, go back to the brief. Who is this logo for? What should it say in three seconds? Where will it live most often, on packaging, on a website, on a uniform? A concept that feels plain to you may be exactly right for a customer who needs to trust the business at a glance.

Personal taste still matters, but it comes second. Start every comment by naming what the logo needs to do, then say whether it does it. "Our buyers are busy parents, and this mark feels too playful for a service they need to trust" is useful. "I just do not like blue" is not, unless blue genuinely clashes with the brand you described.

If the brief itself was wrong, say so now. A designer cannot fix a strategy problem with a color change. Tell them the audience or the message has shifted, and let them rethink the concept instead of polishing a direction that no longer fits.

Name the problem, not the solution

Clients often hand over a solution: "Make the icon bigger, use a serif font, add a swoosh." The trouble is that the solution may fix the wrong problem. A bigger icon does not fix a mark that feels generic. A new font does not fix a layout that feels crowded.

Describe what you see and what it makes you feel instead. "The mark disappears at small sizes" points to a real problem, and the designer can solve it several ways. "The wordmark feels heavier than the icon, so my eye stops at the text" is even better. When you name the problem clearly, you get the designer's best solution, not just your first guess.

The one exception is a hard constraint. If your printer only runs one color, or your app icon must work in a circle, state it as a fact. Constraints narrow the work in a good way. Opinions dressed up as instructions do the opposite.

Pick one direction, then refine it

The fastest way to stall a project is to ask for a mix of three concepts. A little of concept A, the color of concept B, and the font of concept C rarely produces a stronger logo. It produces a compromise that nobody chose on purpose.

Rank the concepts first. Tell the designer which one is closest and why, in one or two sentences. Then list your changes for that direction only, in order of importance. Three clear changes beat ten scattered ones, because the designer knows what matters most if a change forces a tradeoff.

Keep each round focused. Round one is about direction and idea. Round two is about polish: spacing, weight, color, and small details. Trying to settle the idea and the details in the same round is how projects loop for weeks.

Use words that move the work forward

Compare these two notes. "This feels corporate and cold" leaves the designer guessing. "This feels corporate and cold because the sharp angles and dark navy read like a bank, and we need to feel friendly to first time buyers" gives them something to change. The difference is one sentence of reasoning.

A simple test before you send feedback: could a stranger act on this note without calling you? If the answer is no, add the reason. "Too simple" becomes "too simple because at favicon size the mark reads as a plain circle." "Too busy" becomes "too busy because the icon, the tagline, and the pattern compete for attention."

Finally, say what is working too. Designers are not fishing for praise. They need to know which parts to protect while they fix the rest. One line about what to keep can save a good idea from being redesigned out of the next round.

A logo gets better when the client and the designer solve the same problem. Bring the problem, bring the reason, pick one direction, and let the designer do the designing. That is the whole method.
