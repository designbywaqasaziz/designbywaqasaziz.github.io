WHAT TO DO WHEN YOU HATE THE FIRST LOGO CONCEPTS.

By Waqas AzizNovember 23, 20263 min read

You open the first concepts and your stomach drops. Nothing feels right. Before you fire off a reply, stop. Hating the first round is normal. It is also fixable, but only if you handle the next step well.

Do not reply in the first hour

Your first reaction is mostly surprise. You have lived with an idea of your logo in your head for weeks, and the real thing never matches the picture in your head on the first look. Sleep on it. Look again the next morning with fresh eyes. Half the time, the concept you hated at midnight looks like a solid starting point at breakfast. If it still feels wrong after a second look, that feeling is real, and now you can describe it calmly instead of reacting to it. While you wait, write down your raw reactions anyway. Those first notes are useful, even if you never send them, because they show you which part bothered you most before your brain started rationalizing it.

Name exactly what is wrong

"I do not like it" is not feedback. It gives your designer nothing to work with, and it usually leads to a second round that misses in a new way. Point at the specific part. Is it the symbol? The lettering? The feeling: too playful, too corporate, too plain? Say it plainly. "The symbol feels too heavy. The type feels too friendly for a law firm." That kind of sentence is worth more than a page of vague dislike. If one detail does work, say so. A designer needs to know what to keep as much as what to change.

Judge the mark, not the mockup

A concept shown on a business card or a shop sign is easy to misjudge. You might be reacting to the mockup, the colors around it, or the photo behind it, not the logo itself. Ask to see the mark alone, in black on white, at a small size. That is the honest test. If the bare mark still feels wrong, the problem is real. If it suddenly looks better, you were judging the presentation, not the design.

Keep the jury small

Show the concepts to ten people and you will get ten opinions, and most of them will be about personal taste, not your brand. Your cousin likes blue. Your partner likes the third one. None of that helps. Keep the decision with the one or two people who actually understand the business and its customers. A logo is not a popularity contest. It is a tool, and the people who should judge it are the people who know what the tool has to do.

Ask for a direction, not a dozen new ideas

The worst move after a bad first round is asking for six more concepts. More options do not fix an unclear direction. They just spread the work thinner. Instead, pick the closest concept and push it in one clear direction. "Take the second one, make it sharper, and drop the icon." One focused revision round will teach you more about your brand than ten random alternatives. Good designers expect revisions. That is what the rounds are for.

The first round is a conversation, not a verdict. Say what is wrong, say it clearly, and give the process one focused round to respond. Most of the logos people end up loving started as a first round somebody almost rejected. The designers who do this work well are not offended by honest feedback. They are stuck only when the feedback is vague. Give them something real to work with, and the second round will usually be the one that clicks.
