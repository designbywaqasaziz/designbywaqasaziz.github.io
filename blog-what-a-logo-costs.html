WHAT A LOGO ACTUALLY COSTS.

By Waqas AzizOctober 23, 20264 min read

You can pay $5 for a logo or $50,000 for one. Both are called logos. They are not the same product, and knowing the difference will save you from buying the wrong one twice.

The $5 logo is a file, not a brand

At the bottom of the market you are buying a premade template with your name typed into it. It takes the seller minutes and it shows. The same mark gets sold to dozens of businesses, the fonts are generic, and no thinking went into any of it. For a weekend hobby this is fine. For a business that plans to still exist in two years, it is a liability.

Here is the part nobody tells you at this price: you cannot trademark what everyone owns. When a competitor or a random shop in another country shows up with your exact mark, you have no legal ground and no moral ground. Your logo is supposed to be the one asset that is uniquely yours. A $5 logo makes it shared property on day one. Rebranding later costs you far more than doing it properly now, because you pay to rebuild every sign, card, and profile you already shipped.

What the middle tier actually buys you

Most working designers charge somewhere between a few hundred and a few thousand dollars. What you are paying for here is not drawing time. It is research, strategy, and iteration. A proper process goes like this: the designer studies your market, sketches dozens of directions, kills most of them, and refines the survivors into something that works in one color, at small sizes, and next to your competitors. The fee covers the thinking you never see.

This is the sweet spot for most startups. You get original concepts built on real strategy, multiple rounds of refinement, and a final package with the file formats you actually need. The designer asks hard questions about your audience and your positioning before touching a sketch. That questioning is the product. If a designer never asks who your customers are or why you exist, the fee is too high at any price.

Why big identities cost five figures

When a serious company pays $25,000 or more, the logo is maybe ten percent of the bill. The rest is the system: guidelines, application rules, templates, a naming check, trademark support, and weeks of stakeholder workshops. They are not buying a drawing. They are buying certainty that every touchpoint will look like the same company for the next decade.

You do not need this as a startup. The lesson to borrow from it is that the mark is the smallest part of the deliverable. Big clients pay for documentation and governance because they have fifty people who will touch the brand. You have three. Match the investment to the scale of the problem. A lean startup with a strong three-figure identity beats a confused corporation with a six-figure one every time.

Red flags that cost you more than the logo

Price alone does not tell you what you are getting, so watch the process. Red flags: the designer shows you five concepts in an hour, which means templates. No questions about your business, which means guesswork. No contract on ownership, which means you may not legally own what you paid for. And my favorite: unlimited revisions, which is code for the designer has no process and will keep redrawing until you get tired. A confident designer limits rounds because each round has a purpose.

On the other side, do not punish a designer for asking for your budget upfront. It is not greed. It is how they scope the work. A $500 budget and a $5,000 budget buy completely different processes, and knowing which one it is lets them propose something honest instead of improvising.

How to set your budget without guessing

Start from the business, not the price tag. If your brand will carry customer trust, investor money, or premium pricing, the identity deserves real investment. One rule that works: budget roughly what a single good customer is worth to you. If one client brings in $2,000, a $1,000 identity pays for itself the first time it tips a decision in your favor.

Never spend money you need for rent. But never treat the logo as the cheapest line item either. It is the face your money wears. Spend what matches the ambition, verify the process before you verify the price, and you will only buy this once.

Pay for thinking, not for files. The files are the cheapest part of a logo.
