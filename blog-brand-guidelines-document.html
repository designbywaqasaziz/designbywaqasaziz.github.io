WHAT A BRAND GUIDELINES DOCUMENT SHOULD ACTUALLY CONTAIN.

By Waqas AzizNovember 6, 20264 min read

A logo on its own is a promise. A brand guidelines document is what keeps that promise after the designer leaves the room. Without it, every new hire, freelancer, and printer makes their own decisions, and the brand slowly drifts into a dozen different versions of itself. A good guidelines document stops that drift. Here is what it should actually contain.

The logo rules, written down

Start with the logo itself. The document should show every approved version: the primary logo, the stacked and horizontal lockups, the symbol on its own, and the one-color versions. Each one needs a clear job, so nobody has to guess which version fits a website header and which fits a social avatar.

Then come the limits. Minimum size, clear space, and the backgrounds each version may sit on. These sound like small details until someone shrinks the logo onto a pen or stretches it across a banner. Rules here are not fussiness. They are what keep the mark legible in the places it actually has to work.

Include the mistakes too. A short page of do-nots, stretched, recolored, low contrast, or set in the wrong font, saves more arguments than any list of rules. People remember a forbidden example faster than a written instruction.

Color and type, with exact values

Colors need numbers, not names. Dark green means a different shade to every person who reads it. The document should list every brand color with its hex code for screens, its CMYK values for print, and its Pantone reference where it matters. Name the role of each color as well: which one leads, which one supports, and which one is only for small accents.

Typography gets the same treatment. Name the fonts, the weights that are allowed, and the sizes for headings, body text, and captions. Show the hierarchy in action, not just a list of font names. If a free fallback font is needed for documents and emails, name that too, so the brand still sounds like itself in a Word file.

Voice and tone, in plain words

A brand is not only how it looks. It is also how it speaks. The guidelines should describe the voice in a few plain sentences, and give real examples: how the brand writes a headline, how it answers a complaint, how it celebrates a win. Three example sentences teach more than three paragraphs of adjectives.

Keep the language practical. Words like bold and friendly mean nothing until someone can see them in a sentence. Write a short list of words the brand uses and words it never uses. That list alone will keep social posts, emails, and packaging sounding like they came from the same company.

Real examples, not just rules

Rules without examples get ignored. Every section of the document should end with the brand applied to something real: a business card, a social post, a slide, a parcel box. When people see the system working on real pieces, they stop treating the guidelines as theory and start treating them as the way things are done.

This is also where gaps show up. If a rule cannot survive contact with a real layout, the rule is wrong, not the layout. A guidelines document that only works in a presentation is decoration. One that survives real work is a tool.

A brand guidelines document is not a trophy for the launch. It is the manual that keeps the brand honest for the next five years. Build it to be used, keep it short enough to read, and update it when the brand grows. The brands that look consistent are not the ones with the strictest rules. They are the ones whose rules are clear enough that anyone can follow them.
