DESIGN TRENDS THAT ARE ACTUALLY WORTH USING.

By Waqas AzizOctober 25, 20264 min read

Every month a new list of design trends shows up, and every month some founder sends it to me with the same question: should our brand do this? Most of the time my answer is no. Not because trends are bad, but because a trend is not a strategy. A trend is a tool. Used in the right place it makes a brand feel current. Used in the wrong place it makes a brand look like it is wearing someone else's clothes.

Type is doing the talking now

Big, chunky, confident type is everywhere right now, and for once the hype is justified. When a startup cannot afford a photoshoot or a mascot, strong type is the cheapest way to look intentional. A bold wordmark on a plain background says you know what you are selling before a single image loads.

The rule is simple. If you remove the image and the type still carries the message, it works. If the letters overlap so much that nobody can read them, you do not have a design, you have a puzzle. Type as the hero works when it is readable first and stylish second. The moment style wins over reading, the trend is working against you.

The human hand is back, because everything else looks machine made

Hand-drawn marks, loose doodles, sketchy mascots. They are everywhere for one reason: perfect visuals became cheap. When anyone can generate a flawless image in seconds, imperfection became the signal that a human was involved. A wobbly line says someone sat down and drew this. Customers feel that, even if they never put it into words.

Use it where it belongs: packaging, social posts, secondary illustrations, the friendly corners of a brand. Be careful with it as the main logo. A doodle rarely survives at favicon size or in a single color, and a logo has to survive both. Keep the logo clean. Let the hand-drawn work live around it.

Texture is the cheapest depth you can buy

Flat design ruled for a decade, and now everything flat looks the same. Grain, paper feel, a lightly embossed surface, these give a brand depth without adding clutter. A little texture makes a digital brand feel like it exists in the real world, like something you could touch.

The key word is little. Texture should be felt, not noticed. If the first thing someone sees is the grain, you used too much. The best texture work is the kind a customer only notices when it is gone.

Your own culture is a trend no one can copy

The strongest direction I see right now is design rooted in a real visual culture. South Asian truck art and ornament, Arabic calligraphy, local craft patterns. It works because it comes from somewhere. A brand that draws on its own visual language looks confident in a way that borrowed minimalism never will.

There is one hard line here. Borrowing another culture's look as decoration is the fastest way to look fake. If the visual language is not actually yours, leave it alone. Customers can tell the difference between heritage and a costume, and they judge the costume harshly.

Nostalgia only works if the story is real

Film grain, polaroid frames, stamp borders, old postcard layouts. They all sell the same feeling: a time before feeds, when things were made to last. For a brand with real heritage, or a genuinely handmade product, this feeling is honest and it sells.

It fails when a brand with no past pretends to have one. A tech startup dressed as a 1970s postcard does not look charming, it looks confused. Nostalgia is a story, not a filter. If your brand has no such story, do not rent one.

Trends I would skip for most brands

Surveillance style interfaces, empty liminal rooms, faces bent through glass. They grab attention on a poster, and that is exactly the problem. A brand needs trust and clarity more than it needs attention. A customer who cannot tell what you sell in three seconds will not stay to admire the effect.

The same goes for pixel mosaic outside gaming. It is a fun visual idea, but for a skincare brand or a consultancy it answers a question nobody asked. Attention is not the same as understanding, and a confused customer does not buy.

The three question test

Before you follow any trend, ask three questions. First, does it fit your customer, or just your feed? Second, does it survive in one color and at small size? Third, will it still make sense in three years? If a trend fails two out of three, skip it. If it passes all three, it is not really a trend anymore. It is your style.

Trends are other people's answers. Your brand still needs its own. Borrow the tools, not the identity.
